Overview

In this homework, I implemented a real-time mass-spring cloth simulator from scratch. The cloth is discretized as a uniform grid of point masses connected by three types of springs (structural, shearing, and bending) which together model the in-plane stretching, in-plane shearing, and out-of-plane bending stiffness of a piece of fabric. Forces from gravity and Hooke's law springs are accumulated each timestep and integrated using Verlet integration, with Provot's length-correction step to prevent the springs from over-stretching. I then added collisions against analytic primitives (spheres and planes) and self-collisions accelerated by a spatial hash. Finally, in Part 5 I wrote a small library of GLSL shaders — diffuse, Blinn-Phong, texture mapping, bump and displacement mapping, and a mirror-style environment-map reflection — to actually render the cloth nicely.

The most interesting things I learned were (1) just how much the visual realism of cloth comes from the bending springs and the Provot length constraint rather than the integrator itself; (2) how cheap and effective spatial hashing is for self-collisions compared to an O(n²) brute-force pass; and (3) the surprisingly small mathematical difference between bump and displacement mapping in the shader, despite producing completely different silhouettes.

Part 1: Masses and springs

For Part 1, I built a regular num_width_points × num_height_points grid of PointMass objects. The grid is laid out in row-major order (i.e. index j * num_width_points + i). For a HORIZONTAL orientation, all masses sit in the plane y = 1, while for a VERTICAL orientation they lie in the xy plane with a small random z-perturbation in [-0.001, 0.001] so the cloth doesn't start out perfectly flat. Pinned masses are detected by scanning the pinned input and setting pm.pinned = true.

After the masses are created, I make a second pass to add springs. To avoid double-counting, each spring is added only once from the "current" mass to one of its predecessors:

  • Structural: to the mass directly to the left and directly above.
  • Shearing: to the upper-left and upper-right diagonal masses.
  • Bending: to the mass two steps to the left and two steps above.

Without shearing (a), the grid still has a clean rectangular weave but lacks the diagonal cross-bracing. With all three (c), every cell has its full set of axis-aligned, diagonal, and skip-one connections.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

The simulation step has three pieces. First I compute total force on every point mass: external acceleration a contributes Fext = m·a, and each enabled spring contributes a Hookean force F = ks(‖pa − pb‖ − L0) along the spring axis, applied with opposite signs to the two endpoints. As the spec suggests, bending springs use 0.2 * ks because real cloth bends much more easily than it stretches.

Second, I update positions with Verlet integration (no explicit velocities):

\[ x_{t+1} = x_t + (1 - d)(x_t - x_{t-1}) + a \, \Delta t^2 \]

where d is the damping fraction and a = F / m. Pinned masses are skipped.

Finally, I apply the Provot length-correction pass: any spring that has stretched more than 10% past its rest length is shortened back to 1.1 · L0. The correction is split evenly between the two endpoints, except when one endpoint is pinned (the other absorbs the full correction) or when both are pinned (the spring is left alone).

Effect of ks

With a very low ks (e.g. 100 N/m), the cloth becomes extremely stretchy: it sags dramatically under its own weight, and the Provot 10% cap is the only thing keeping it from collapsing into a string. With a very high ks (e.g. 50000 N/m), the cloth is almost rigid — it barely deforms at all between the pins and reaches its resting shape very quickly with very little oscillation.

Effect of density

Density controls the mass per area, so it also controls the gravitational force (F = m·g) on each mass. With a low density (e.g. 1), the cloth is light and almost floats — it falls slowly and barely deforms the springs. With a very high density (e.g. 100), each mass is heavier, gravity dominates, and the cloth sags significantly even with the same ks, in a way that visually resembles a low-ks case.

Effect of damping

Damping controls how much of the previous frame's velocity carries forward via the (1 − d) factor in Verlet. With low damping (close to 0), the cloth oscillates almost forever; you can watch it swing like a pendulum well past when it should have settled. With very high damping (close to 1), motion is killed almost instantly — the cloth falls into its rest pose smoothly without any visible bouncing.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Sphere collisions. Given a point mass at position p and a sphere at o with radius r, I check whether ‖p − o‖ < r. If so, the point has penetrated the sphere. I project it back to the closest point on the surface by computing the tangent point t = o + r · (p − o)/‖p − o‖, then form the correction vector from the mass's last_position to this tangent point and scale it by (1 − f), where f is the friction coefficient. The new position becomes last_position + (1 − f) * correction. Because Verlet derives velocity from (position − last_position), scaling the correction this way naturally damps the tangential velocity, which is exactly what kinetic friction does.

Plane collisions. For a plane defined by a point and normal, I detect a crossing by checking whether (plast − point)·n and (p − point)·n have opposite signs — i.e. the point traveled through the plane during this timestep. If so, I project p back to the plane and offset it by SURFACE_OFFSET along the normal on whichever side plast was originally on. Then the same friction-scaled correction from last_position is applied as in the sphere case.

Cloth on sphere with varying ks

With ks = 500 the cloth is very compliant and drapes tightly around the sphere, hugging its surface and forming many small, soft folds where it pools below. At the default ks = 5000 the cloth makes wider, smoother folds and partially holds its shape. With ks = 50000 the cloth is so stiff that it barely conforms to the sphere at all — instead of wrapping snugly, it stays mostly flat on top with sharp creases where it bends over the sphere's silhouette, like a stiff piece of paper.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

For self-collisions I built a uniform spatial hash. Each point mass's 3D position is bucketed into a box of dimensions (3·w/nw, 3·h/nh, max(...)) by a hash_position function that floors each coordinate to the nearest box corner and combines them with three large primes (31, 53, 97) to produce a single float key. build_spatial_map rebuilds this hash table once per simulation step.

For each point mass, self_collide looks up its bucket and iterates over candidate neighbors. For any neighbor closer than 2 · thickness (and not the mass itself), I accumulate a corrective vector that pushes the two apart so they end up exactly 2 · thickness apart. After visiting all candidates, the average correction is divided by simulation_steps and applied to the point's position. The averaging-and-scaling avoids "fighting" between conflicting corrections within a single step, so the cloth settles smoothly instead of jittering.

Cloth folding on itself over time

You can see the cloth begin to crumple as soon as the lower section catches the upper section, then continue folding into progressively smaller and tighter creases as more of the cloth lands on top of itself.

Effect of density on self-collision

Higher density makes each layer of cloth heavier, so the upper layers compress the lower ones and the resulting folds are tighter and flatter. Lower density gives a more "fluffy" pile because the cloth has less weight to push the folds closed.

Effect of ks on self-collision

A low ks lets the cloth bend at a very small radius, so it crinkles into many fine folds. A high ks resists bending sharply and produces a small number of large, smooth folds — the cloth behaves more like cardboard than fabric.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a small program that runs on the GPU as part of the rasterization pipeline. The two shader stages I worked with are the vertex shader, which runs once per vertex and is responsible for transforming each vertex from object space into clip space (and passing useful per-vertex data — position, normal, tangent, UV — to the next stage), and the fragment shader, which runs once per pixel covered by a triangle and computes that pixel's final color. The two stages communicate through interpolated out/in variables: the rasterizer interpolates each out value from the three vertices of the triangle using barycentric coordinates and hands the result to the fragment shader as in. This is how a vertex shader passing world-space positions and normals lets the fragment shader run a per-pixel lighting calculation.

Blinn-Phong

Blinn-Phong is a local lighting model that approximates the appearance of a surface as the sum of three terms:

\[ L = k_a I_a + k_d \frac{I}{r^2} \max(0, n \cdot l) + k_s \frac{I}{r^2} \max(0, n \cdot h)^p \]

The ambient term (ka Ia) is a flat fill that approximates indirect light bouncing around the scene; it's the color you see in shadow. The diffuse term models matte Lambertian reflection — it depends on the angle between the surface normal n and the light direction l, and is independent of view angle. The specular term models the bright highlight from a glossy surface using the half-vector h = (l + v)/‖l + v‖; the exponent p controls how tight the highlight is. Both diffuse and specular fall off with the inverse square of the distance to the light. I used the parameters ka = 0.1, kd = 1.0, ks = 0.5, p = 100.

Bump and displacement mapping

Bump mapping perturbs only the shading normal: the surface geometry is unchanged, but I sample the height map's local gradient and use a TBN matrix to rotate a perturbed normal no = (−dU, −dV, 1) from tangent space into world space, then feed it through the same Blinn-Phong calculation. Displacement mapping does the same shading but also moves each vertex along its normal by h(uv) · scale in the vertex shader — actually changing the geometry that gets rasterized.

The two look almost identical when the silhouette is small or the mesh is dense, but they differ in two important ways: (1) bump mapping leaves the silhouette of the object perfectly smooth, while displacement creates real bumps along the silhouette; and (2) bump mapping is independent of mesh density, while displacement is fundamentally limited by how many vertices you have to move.

Coarse vs. fine sphere mesh

This is the cleanest way to see the difference between the two techniques. With -o 16 -a 16, the bump-mapped sphere still looks reasonably bumpy because the lighting calculation is per-pixel and unaffected by triangle count, but the displacement-mapped sphere looks blocky and faceted along its silhouette because there are simply not enough vertices to displace into the bump pattern. At -o 128 -a 128 both look great, and the displacement version even shows real bumpy silhouettes that bump mapping can never produce.

Mirror

For the mirror shader I compute the view direction wo = vposition − ucam_pos, reflect it about the surface normal using GLSL's reflect(wo, n), and use the resulting direction to sample the cubemap u_texture_cubemap. The output color is whatever the environment looks like in that reflected direction.
